## The Expectation Has Changed

Five years ago, "business English" at many HCMC companies meant writing formal emails. That bar has moved.

International clients now expect video calls. Foreign-managed teams run standup meetings in English. Report presentations happen live, not written and submitted. The speaking requirement—specifically *impromptu* speaking—has risen sharply.

## What "Good Enough" Actually Looks Like

I ask my adult students to define what they mean by "improve my English". The answer is usually one of three things:

1. Emails and written communication
2. Meetings and phone calls with foreign colleagues
3. Interviews or professional presentations

Each has a different skill requirement. If you're not clear on which one you need, you'll spend time on the wrong thing.

**Emails**: Grammar accuracy and tone matter most. Fluency matters less—you have time to draft and edit.

**Meetings**: Real-time listening comprehension and short, clear responses. You don't need perfect grammar. You need speed and confidence.

**Presentations**: Structure and delivery. The content needs to be organised. The delivery needs to project confidence even when you're nervous.

## The Gap That Costs People Jobs

The most common gap I see: people who read and write English confidently but freeze when asked something unexpected in a conversation.

This is a speaking practice problem, not a knowledge problem. The solution is not more study. It's more conversation—uncomfortable, unscripted, real-time conversation—until the freeze stops happening.

## A Practical Starting Point

If you're working in English and feeling out of depth in meetings:

- Prepare three standard phrases for buying yourself thinking time: "That's a good point—can I just confirm..." / "Let me make sure I'm following you..." / "Could you say that once more?"
- Stop translating in your head before speaking. Start with the English word you have, even if imperfect.
- Ask a foreign colleague to tell you one thing you said that wasn't clear, once a week. Most will appreciate the directness.

The fear of being understood imperfectly is usually worse than actually being imperfect. People communicate across language gaps all the time. The key is keeping the conversation going.
