## Starting Out

I came to Ho Chi Minh City three years ago with a TESOL certification, some teaching experience from Uzbekistan, and a fairly confident set of assumptions about what teaching here would look like.

Most of those assumptions were wrong.

## What I Got Wrong: Silence Means Confusion

In my early classes, silence after a question made me nervous. I assumed students didn't understand.

Often, they understood perfectly. They were thinking. Or they were waiting to see if someone else would volunteer first. Or they were calculating the social risk of being wrong in front of peers.

Vietnamese classroom culture has a strong norm around not publicly exposing uncertainty. Students who would enthusiastically answer in a one-to-one setting would say nothing in a group. I spent two months misreading this as comprehension failure when it was mostly social calibration.

**What I learned**: Wait longer after questions. Reduce the social stakes of answering. Ask for written responses before verbal ones. Give students time to check with a partner before speaking to the group.

## What I Got Wrong: English Errors Are Random

I thought errors would be fairly distributed—random mistakes reflecting partial knowledge.

They're not. Vietnamese learners share a highly consistent set of error patterns: final consonant dropping, article omission (Vietnamese has no direct equivalent), aspect/tense confusion (Vietnamese expresses time through context rather than verb changes), and a few others.

Once I understood these patterns, I could anticipate and address them systematically rather than reactively.

## What I Got Wrong: More Content Is Better Lesson Planning

I arrived with detailed lesson plans packed with content. I thought: thorough preparation, good teaching.

In practice, the most useful lessons were often the most sparse. One grammar point. Fifteen minutes of varied practice. Extended speaking time. Done.

Vietnamese students—particularly younger ones—retain much more from a focused lesson with repetition than from a varied lesson covering four points. I now routinely plan less and slow down more.

## What I Got Right

Students here are extraordinarily motivated when they see a reason to learn. Parents are deeply invested in their children's progress. Feedback is taken seriously. If I give a student a correction and a method, they'll practise it.

That consistency—the genuine effort students bring—makes teaching here one of the more rewarding environments I've worked in.

The mistakes were mine to fix. The students did their part.
