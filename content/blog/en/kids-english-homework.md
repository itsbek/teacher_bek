## The Worry I Hear Often

"My English isn't good. How can I help my child?"

This is one of the most common questions parents ask me—and it's worth answering properly, because the answer is more encouraging than people expect.

You don't need to speak English to be enormously useful.

## What Actually Helps

### Consistency of Routine

Children learn fastest when they practise regularly. You don't need to correct their pronunciation. You need to ensure they sit down and do the work—same time, same place, four or five days a week.

Fifteen minutes of daily practice beats two hours on Saturday.

### Showing Interest Without Pressure

Ask your child to teach you one English word each day. This does two things: it reinforces their recall, and it tells them you value what they're learning.

You don't have to understand the word. Just ask.

### Creating a Low-Stress Environment

Children absorb language much faster when they feel safe to be wrong. If homework time is associated with stress or frustration, the brain partly shuts down.

Your job isn't to correct—it's to make the environment calm and positive. That is teaching support, even if it doesn't feel like it.

### Communicating with the Teacher

If your child is struggling, let the teacher know. You don't need to diagnose the problem—just describe what you see. "She's spending an hour on each assignment" or "He gets anxious before class" is extremely useful feedback.

## What Doesn't Help

- Hovering over the homework and correcting every word
- Comparing your child to other students
- Rewarding or punishing based on grades rather than effort
- Stopping practice when they "seem fine"

Progress in language learning is often invisible for weeks at a time before it suddenly jumps. Consistency during the flat periods is what makes the jump happen.

---

Parents who can't speak English often produce some of the most motivated language learners I've taught—because the child wants to close the gap. That's a powerful driver.
