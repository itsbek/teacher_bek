## The problem with big classes

In a class of 20, each student gets roughly three minutes of speaking time per hour. Three minutes. The rest is listening, waiting, or zoning out.

That's not a teaching problem — it's a math problem.

## What changes at four students

When a group drops to four, the math flips entirely. Each student speaks for roughly fifteen minutes every hour. More importantly, they can't hide. There's nowhere to sit quietly and avoid being called on.

That pressure is uncomfortable at first. Then it becomes the reason they improve.

The other shift is correction. In a big class, I can't stop to address every mistake — the lesson grinds to a halt. In a small group, I can note errors in real time, return to them at the end, and make sure the correction lands personally.

> The goal isn't comfort. It's growth just fast enough to feel like progress.

## What I've seen in practice

I've taught both formats across fifteen schools in Ho Chi Minh City. The pattern is consistent: students in small groups reach conversational fluency in roughly half the time of students in standard classes, assuming similar starting levels and attendance.

The reason isn't the teacher. It's the hours of actual output.

## What this means for you

If you're choosing between a cheaper big class and a more expensive small one, the question isn't really about cost — it's about how many months of your time you're willing to trade.

Small groups cost more per hour. They cost significantly less per result.
