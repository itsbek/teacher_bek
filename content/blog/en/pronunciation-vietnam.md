## Accent vs. Clarity

Having a Vietnamese accent is not a problem. Clarity is the goal—being understood without effort by your listener. Most Vietnamese learners have a handful of consistent patterns that reduce clarity. Address those, and everything else sounds significantly better.

## 1. Final Consonant Dropping

Vietnamese words rarely end in consonant sounds. As a result, Vietnamese speakers often drop the final consonant in English words.

- "Stopped" sounds like "stop"
- "Talked" sounds like "talk"
- "Test" sounds like "tes"

This affects both comprehension (listeners miss the word) and grammar (past tense, plurals, third-person singular all rely on final sounds).

**Fix**: At the end of every word, pause for one beat after the final consonant. Exaggerate it in practice. "Stop-t. Walk-t." It will feel strange. Do it anyway.

## 2. Vowel Length — Short and Long Confusion

English has short and long vowel sounds that Vietnamese doesn't distinguish. "Ship" and "sheep" sound the same to many Vietnamese ears—and therefore to their mouths.

Other pairs: "it / eat", "full / fool", "bit / beat"

**Fix**: Exaggerate the length. When you mean "sheep", hold the vowel for twice as long as you think necessary. Your listeners will thank you.

## 3. Stress on the Wrong Syllable

English word stress is not fixed in position the way Vietnamese tones are. "PHOtograph" vs "phoTOgraphy" vs "photoGRAPHic"—same root, three different stress positions.

Vietnamese learners often apply even stress to every syllable, which sounds robotic and makes words harder to recognise.

**Fix**: When you learn a new word, learn where the stress falls at the same time. Mark it. Practise it. Word lists without stress marks are half-useful at best.

---

These three patterns cover the majority of clarity issues I hear. Work on one at a time. Start with final consonants—it's the one with the biggest immediate payoff.
