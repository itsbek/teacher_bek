## Two Kinds of Safety

When I talk about a safe classroom for children, I mean two different things—and parents usually focus on the first while underestimating the second.

**Physical safety**: CCTV, clean facilities, a secure building, mosquito control. These matter. Parents need peace of mind about the physical environment, especially for younger children.

**Psychological safety**: The classroom culture that tells students their errors won't be mocked, their questions are welcome, and slow progress is normal. This is what actually determines whether a child learns.

## Why Psychological Safety Accelerates Learning

Language acquisition requires production—you have to try to speak, which means you have to risk being wrong. In a classroom where errors feel dangerous (socially, emotionally), children go quiet. They wait to be sure before speaking.

Waiting to be sure before speaking is the opposite of what language learning requires.

Children who feel psychologically safe:
- Attempt answers rather than staying silent
- Ask for clarification when they don't understand
- Take risks with new vocabulary
- bounce back from corrections without shutting down

All of these behaviours speed up acquisition dramatically.

## What I Do to Build This

**No public correction by name.** When a student makes an error, I correct the language pattern—not the child. "Good try. We usually say 'went', not 'goed'." The class hears the correct form. No one is singled out.

**Praise effort, not accuracy.** "That was a great attempt" matters more than "That was correct." Accuracy follows effort. The reverse is not reliable.

**Normalise not knowing.** I don't know some Vietnamese words. I show students that and move on. Not knowing is not failure—it's just a current state.

**Slow the correction loop.** I don't jump on every error. Some errors I log and address later, globally. Students speak more freely when they're not being corrected every sentence.

## For Parents

If your child comes home and says they don't want to go to class, the first question isn't "what are they not understanding?" It's "do they feel safe being wrong there?"

Ask them: *Did you say something in class today? How did the teacher respond?*

The answer tells you more than a test score.
