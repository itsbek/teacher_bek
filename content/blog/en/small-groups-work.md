## The Maths Are Simple

I used to work in large language centres. Classes of 20, sometimes 30 students. I'd watch students sit quietly, waiting for their turn—which often never came.

Run the numbers: a 60-minute lesson, 25 students, some explanation time, some group work. Each student gets roughly **3 to 5 minutes of actual speaking time**.

That is not a language class. That is an observation session.

## What Changes with Ten or Fewer

When the group is small:

- Every student speaks in every lesson
- Mistakes get caught and corrected in the moment
- Quieter students can't hide—and don't need to
- The teacher can track each person's progress week to week

The difference isn't the content or the curriculum. It's the air time.

## Speaking is a Skill, Not Knowledge

You can study grammar for years and still freeze when a foreigner asks you for directions. Speaking is a *physical* skill, like swimming. You get better by doing it—repeatedly, under real pressure, with feedback.

Small groups create that environment. Big classes largely don't.

## What I Do

My groups stay at ten maximum. That's not a marketing point—it's a practical decision. Above ten, the quality of speaking practice drops noticeably. Below five, you lose the dynamic of multiple voices and accents in the room.

Ten is the number that works.

If you're looking for a class where you'll actually speak, that's worth knowing upfront.
